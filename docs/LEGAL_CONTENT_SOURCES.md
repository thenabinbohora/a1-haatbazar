## Privacy and the Australian Privacy Principles

### OAIC — Small business

Source: [Small business](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business)

- Most businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less are not covered by the *Privacy Act 1988*, but important exceptions can apply regardless of turnover.
- Coverage can depend on matters such as trading in personal information, providing certain health services, Commonwealth contracting, relationships with covered organisations, or opting in.
- A1 Haat Bazar's turnover, exceptions and opt-in status have not been legally verified, so the public draft should not claim that the business is—or is not—an APP entity.
- If the Privacy Act applies, the business must comply with the APPs. The OAIC also recommends protecting personal information as good practice where the Act does not apply.

### OAIC — Australian Privacy Principles guidance

Sources:

- [Australian Privacy Principles guidelines](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines)
- [Read the Australian Privacy Principles](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/read-the-australian-privacy-principles)
- [Chapter 1: APP 1 — Open and transparent management of personal information](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-1-app-1-open-and-transparent-management-of-personal-information)

- If A1 Haat Bazar is APP-covered, it must maintain a clearly expressed, current privacy policy.
- An APP privacy policy should explain the kinds of personal information collected and held; how it is collected and held; collection, use and disclosure purposes; access and correction; complaint handling; and likely overseas disclosures, including countries where practicable.
- Privacy disclosures should reflect actual information flows and service-provider arrangements. They should not name unverified providers or claim that information remains in Australia unless that has been confirmed.
- APP 8 addresses cross-border disclosure. APP 11 addresses security and destruction or de-identification. APPs 12 and 13 address access to and correction of personal information.
- APP 1 guidance supports describing outsourced storage and service-provider categories while avoiding security details that could weaken the effectiveness of safeguards.

### OAIC — Information security and retention

Source: [Guide to securing personal information](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/handling-personal-information/guide-to-securing-personal-information)

- APP-covered entities must take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorised access, modification or disclosure.
- Information stored by a third-party provider may still be held by the business where the business retains possession or control of it.
- Personal information that is no longer needed should be destroyed or de-identified, subject to lawful retention requirements.
- The policy should describe only verified, high-level safeguards. It should not expose sensitive security configurations, invent retention periods, or promise immediate deletion where records must be retained for legal, accounting, dispute-resolution or fraud-prevention purposes.

## Australian Consumer Law

### ACCC — Consumer guarantees

Source: [Consumer rights and guarantees](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees)

- Consumer guarantees are automatic and cannot be removed by store terms, disclaimers, warranties or “no refunds” wording.
- Products must be of acceptable quality, including being safe, durable and free from defects. They must also be fit for a disclosed purpose and match their description.
- Product-image, packaging, ingredient and labelling qualifications must not be used to override consumer guarantees or excuse inaccurate product information.
- There is no single fixed period for consumer guarantees. What is reasonable depends on the product and the circumstances, so the policy must not impose an arbitrary expiry date on statutory rights.

### ACCC — Repairs, replacements and refunds

Source: [Repair, replace, refund, cancel](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/problem-with-a-product-or-service-you-bought/repair-replace-refund-cancel)

- A major product problem includes an unsafe product, a product materially different from its description or sample, a serious problem that would have prevented purchase, or a product that cannot perform its normal or disclosed purpose and cannot readily be fixed.
- For a major product problem, the consumer generally chooses between a refund and a replacement of the same type. A refund should be for the full amount paid and ordinarily use the original payment method unless the parties agree otherwise.
- A minor problem must be fixed without charge. If it cannot be fixed within a reasonable time, additional remedies may become available.
- The seller is responsible for handling claims and cannot simply direct the consumer to the manufacturer.
- A business may assess a product and request reasonable proof of purchase. Faulty goods do not have to be in their original packaging.
- When a fault is confirmed, reasonable return costs already paid by the consumer must be reimbursed.
- A change of mind alone does not create a consumer-guarantee remedy, but a business must honour any voluntary change-of-mind policy it offers. No unverified change-of-mind policy should be added to the public draft.

### ACCC — Claim handling and online return policies

Sources:

- [Contacting a business to fix a problem](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/problem-with-a-product-or-service-you-bought/contacting-a-business-to-fix-a-problem)
- [ACCC sweep uncovers concerning online shopping return policies and terms and conditions](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/accc-sweep-uncovers-concerning-online-shopping-return-policies-and-terms-and-conditions)

- Original-packaging requirements, short deadlines and other conditions attached to a voluntary change-of-mind policy cannot be applied to statutory claims for faulty or non-compliant products.
- An order number, invoice, card statement or copy of a receipt may support proof of purchase; the original receipt should not be treated as the only acceptable evidence.
- Photos and order details may assist an assessment, but should not be framed as absolute conditions that extinguish statutory rights.
- Blanket exclusions for opened or sale products, mandatory restocking fees, and unconditional statements that delivery charges are non-refundable may mislead consumers about their rights.
- Delivery charges may be recoverable where they form reasonably foreseeable loss caused by a consumer-guarantee failure; the policy should not exclude them in all circumstances.

## Electronic Marketing

### ACMA — Spam Act compliance

Source: [Avoid sending spam](https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam)

- Marketing emails and messages require the recipient's consent. Express consent is best practice, and the business should keep a record of who consented, when, and how.
- A one-off purchase does not, by itself, establish inferred consent to marketing.
- A commercial message must accurately identify the sender, provide current contact details and contain an easy-to-use unsubscribe facility.
- Unsubscribe requests must be actioned within five working days, without a fee, additional personal-information requirement, or forced account login. The facility must remain functional for at least 30 days after the message is sent.
- A business cannot send an electronic marketing message merely to request consent, and remains responsible when a third party sends marketing on its behalf.

### ACMA — Transactional and promotional messages

Source: [Telemarketing and e-marketing — common issues and mistakes](https://www.acma.gov.au/telemarketing-and-e-marketing-common-issues-and-mistakes)

- Checkout, enquiry and electronic-receipt addresses should not be added automatically to a marketing list.
- An order confirmation, shipping notice, password reset or other service message may become commercial if it contains promotional material or links to sales content.
- Essential order, pickup, delivery and account communications should be kept separate from optional marketing.
- Marketing consent and unsubscribe controls should be verified before the privacy policy claims that promotional messaging is available.

## Business guidance

### business.gov.au — Australian Consumer Law and ecommerce

Sources:

- [Australian Consumer Law and your business](https://business.gov.au/legal/fair-trading/australian-consumer-law-and-your-business)
- [Ecommerce (selling online)](https://business.gov.au/online-and-digital/ecommerce)

- Businesses selling goods or services in Australia must comply with the Australian Consumer Law.
- Product and service information must be truthful. Consumer contracts should be clear, understandable and free from hidden fees or unfair terms.
- Store policies cannot override consumer guarantees.
- Online terms should be accurate, easy to find and clear about applicable consumer rights. Customer information and payment methods should be handled securely.
- A1 Haat Bazar's terms should disclose verified prices, fees and order-confirmation steps without naming an unverified payment provider.

### business.gov.au — Receipts and proof of purchase

Source: [Receipts and proof of purchase](https://business.gov.au/finance/payments-and-invoicing/receipts-and-proof-of-purchase)

- A business may ask for proof of purchase when assessing a request for a repair, replacement or refund.
- Proof does not have to be the original receipt. It may include an invoice, card statement, internet order or reference number, or a copy or photograph of a receipt.
- Because the registered entity name and ABN or ACN have not been verified, compliant receipt identity and configuration require owner and professional review rather than invented public details.

## Drafting conclusion

These sources support privacy wording that is conditional on verified legal coverage, returns wording that preserves all non-excludable Australian Consumer Law rights, and marketing wording that distinguishes essential service communications from consent-based promotions. Operational facts, provider names, overseas processing locations, retention periods, payment methods and voluntary return rules must remain limited to information verified from the application or confirmed by the business owner and professional adviser.
